\begin{abstract}
The active galactic nucleus NGC 1068, recently identified as a high-energy
neutrino source by IceCube, exhibits a GeV $\gamma$-ray flux that exceeds
predictions from opaque corona cascade models. This tension suggests the
existence of non-attenuated energy transport mechanisms from the compact
core. We investigate whether heavy neutral leptons (HNLs), coupled to the
Standard Model through mixing with muon neutrinos, can provide such a
channel, and we derive the resulting \emph{exclusionary constraint} on
this HNL scenario from the multi-messenger observations of NGC 1068. We
calculate the production of HNLs in charged-pion and muon decays associated
with hadronic interactions, explicitly tracking the mass-dependent
polarization redistribution. We then derive the spectrum from the visible
$N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$ decay channel and evaluate its secondary
electromagnetic emission. We find that the predicted photon flux is at least
\numrange{7}{8} orders of magnitude below the observational upper limits
once experimental bounds on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ are applied, which excludes
this HNL channel as the origin of the $\gamma$-ray excess. The 
polarization-tracking framework developed in this work is applicable not 
only to astrophysical studies but may also provide useful theoretical 
input for decay simulations, detection-efficiency analyses, and mass 
reconstruction in future accelerator-based HNL searches.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{HNL; Inverse Seesaw; multi-messenger; NGC 1068}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Multi-messenger astronomy has turned astrophysical accelerators into
laboratories for physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM). The IceCube
collaboration has identified the active galactic nucleus (AGN) NGC 1068 as a
point source of neutrinos at energies around \qty{1}{TeV},\cite{icecube2022evidence} and the KM3NeT/ARCA telescope has reported a
high-energy event candidate possibly reaching \qty{220}{PeV}.\cite{km3net2025observation} These observations indicate that hadronic
acceleration operates in at least some AGN cores, and they constrain the neutrino
flux that any transport mechanism in such a source must reproduce.

The same source is anomalous in $\gamma$-rays. The GeV $\gamma$-ray flux of
NGC 1068 measured by Fermi-LAT exceeds the predictions of opaque corona
cascade models, in which the $\gamma$ rays are generated in the optically
thick core and attenuated before escape.\cite{Acciari_2019,murase2020hidden}
The excess therefore suggests a transport channel that
carries energy out of the compact core without being absorbed, and that
converts this energy into observable photons only beyond the opaque region.
Neutral, weakly interacting particles are plausible candidates for such a
channel: they escape the core without electromagnetic attenuation and
decay or scatter in the surrounding environment, where their secondaries 
radiate. The question we address is
whether a specific BSM particle can play this role.

HNLs are motivated here on two levels. Theoretically, the non-zero but
extremely small active neutrino masses require physics beyond the Standard
Model.\cite{ASAKA200517, Boyarsky_2019} HNLs are among the simplest
mediators of neutrino mass and can simultaneously provide a dark-matter candidate and
a target for laboratory searches.\cite{PhysRevLett.103.241802} In the
Inverse Seesaw (ISS) mechanism the smallness of the light neutrino mass is
controlled by a small lepton-number-violating parameter rather than by a
very large Majorana mass, so that the mixing with active neutrinos can
remain large enough to be phenomenologically relevant. Phenomenologically,
a MeV-scale HNL mixed with the muon neutrino can be produced in the
principal neutrino production chain of NGC 1068 (pion and muon decays) and
possesses a visible decay channel, making it a plausible candidate for the
transport channel of this source. Terrestrial experiments have not yet
observed a definite HNL signal, but they constrain the mixing with the muon
neutrino to $|U_{\mu N}|^2 \lesssim 10^{-4}$ for
\qty{3}{MeV} $< m_N <$ \qty{19.5}{MeV};\cite{bryman2019constraints} the
bound is slightly weaker but of the same order over the
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range considered here. These considerations motivate
HNLs as a representative candidate; other neutral candidates, such as
axion-like particles, have been studied separately for
NGC 1068,\cite{pant2024constraining} and we do not attempt a comparative
survey here.

A dedicated calculation is therefore required. A quantitative statement
cannot rely on the massless-neutrino approximation: for $m_N$ in the
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range the HNL is non-relativistic in the parent rest
frame, phase-space suppression is significant, and the helicity produced in
the weak decay is redistributed by the boost in a mass-dependent way. This
is already visible at the production stage: as shown in
Figs.~(\ref{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist})--(\ref{fig:muon_decay_spec}) below,
right-handed HNLs, which are absent in the massless limit, become an
appreciable fraction of the pion-decay yield as $m_N$ approaches the
phase-space boundary, and Fig.~(\ref{fig:muon_decay_spec}) shows the same
effect in muon decay. These polarization states are retained in the
subsequent decay: the visible channel $N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$ inherits
the helicity through the angular distributions $g_e^{(i)}(x,\cos\theta)$ of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:g_e_p2}--\eqref{eq:g_e_p3}, so that the electron spectrum,
and hence the $\gamma$-ray yield, depends on the polarization of the parent
HNL. Polarization-resolved treatments of HNL production and decay have been
developed for accelerator-based HNL searches,\cite{Ballett_2020} but their
application to an astrophysical source requires a source-specific production
spectrum and environment. A polarization-resolved treatment is therefore adopted here to convert
the NGC 1068 multi-messenger data into a quantitative statement about this
channel, and it also makes the framework reusable for other sources and
other HNL mass windows.

In this paper we test whether this HNL channel can account for the
$\gamma$-ray excess of NGC 1068, and we derive the corresponding
exclusionary constraint on this scenario. We compute the
HNL source spectrum from $\pi^\pm \to \mu^\pm + N$ and
$\mu^\pm \to N + e^\pm + \bar{\nu}_e$, separating the two polarization states
and boosting to the laboratory frame while tracking the polarization
redistribution. We then derive the electron/positron spectrum from the
visible decay $N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$, propagate these leptons in a
representative environment of NGC 1068, and compute the resulting
electromagnetic emission with GAMERA \cite{Hahn:2016CO}. Comparing with the
observed $\gamma$-ray flux, we find that the predicted photon flux lies at
least \numrange{7}{8} orders of magnitude below the observational upper
limits once the experimental bound on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ is applied. The HNL
channel is therefore excluded as the origin of the $\gamma$-ray excess.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:iss_model} gives the HNL
coupling, the ISS parameter range, the single-Dirac approximation, and the
decay channels. Section~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay} presents the
polarization-resolved calculations of HNL production in pion and muon decays
and of the visible three-body decay $N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$, and
collects them into the electron source function injected into the radiation
calculation. Section~\ref{sec:HNLs_in_NGC_1068} applies this source function
to the environment of NGC 1068, compares with observations, and gives the
suppression budget and its robustness. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}
summarizes our conclusions.

\section{ISS Model: Parameters and Prerequisites}\label{sec:iss_model}

This section provides the four ingredients needed below: the coupling of
HNLs to the SM, the allowed ranges of $m_N$ and $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ in the ISS, the
justification of the single-Dirac approximation, and the visible decay
channel with its branching ratio. The production and decay calculations of
Sec.~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay} and the experimental bound and
suppression budget of Sec.~\ref{sec:HNLs_in_NGC_1068} rest on them.

\subsection{Coupling}

HNLs interact with the SM only through their mixing with the active
neutrinos. Therefore any process that produces $\nu_\mu$ also produces $N$,
with a rate multiplied by $|U_{\mu N}|^2$, provided that it is
kinematically allowed. Section~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay}
uses this to compute $N$ production in charged-pion and muon decay chains.

\subsection{ISS and parameter ranges}

In the Type-I seesaw:\cite{mohapatra1980neutrino,minkowski1977mu,yanagida1980horizontal,gellmann2013complexspinorsunifiedtheories,glashow1980future,Casas_2001}
\begin{equation}
  m_\nu = \frac{m_D^2}{M_R}, \; m_N = M_R, \; |U|^2 = \left( \frac{m_D}{M_R} \right)^2
\end{equation}
For an
observable $M_R$ the mixing is usually too small to be probed. The Inverse
Seesaw (ISS) \cite{mohapatra1980neutrino,minkowski1977mu,yanagida1980horizontal,gellmann2013complexspinorsunifiedtheories,glashow1980future,Casas_2001}
introduces another fermion field $S$, and the mass matrix reads
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \overline{\nu_L} & \overline{\nu_R^c} & \overline{S^c}
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0   & m_D & 0   \\
    m_D & 0   & M   \\
    0   & M   & \mu
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_L^c \\
    \nu_R   \\
    S
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is a small lepton-number-violating mass term.
For $\mu\ll M$ and $\mu\ll m_D$,
\begin{equation}
  m_\nu = \mu \frac{m_D^2}{M^2}, \; m_N = M, \; |U|^2 = \frac{m_\nu}{\mu}
\end{equation}
The key point is that the smallness of the light neutrino mass is controlled
by the small parameter $\mu$ rather than by a very large $M$, so that for
comparable neutrino masses the mixing can be larger than in the Type-I case.

For the parameters used here, $|U|^2$ can reach the $10^{-4}$ level: taking
$m_\nu\sim0.05$~eV and $\mu\sim\mathrm{keV}$ gives
$|U|^2=m_\nu/\mu\sim10^{-4}$, while $\mu\ll m_D,M$ is satisfied. This is
comparable to the experimental bound quoted in Sec.~\ref{sec:intro} and
motivates the benchmark mixing adopted below.

\subsection{Single-Dirac approximation}

For small $\mu$ the heavy states form a nearly degenerate pseudo-Dirac pair.\cite{Das_2013,Deppisch_2015}
Over the range $m_N \simeq \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV}$ and $\mu \ll m_D,M$
considered here, the mass splitting is much smaller than $m_N$, so that
production and decay cannot resolve the two states; summing over helicities
is therefore equivalent to a single Dirac fermion. The helicity-resolved
calculation of Sec.~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay}, with $s = \pm 1$, rests on
this approximation.

\subsection{Decay channels and visible branching ratio}

Because the HNLs considered here are lighter than the pion, their production
and decay are governed by low-energy weak processes. They decay only into
leptons: a three-neutrino channel (invisible) and a $\nu_\mu e^+e^-$ channel
(visible). Section~\ref{sec:visible_decay} gives the explicit value
$BR \simeq 0.11$, which enters the suppression budget of
Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}.

\section{Polarization-Resolved HNL Production and Decay}
\label{sec:HNL_production_decay}

Under AGN conditions, high-energy cosmic rays undergo $p-p$ and $p-\gamma$
interactions, which are thought to be the main sources of secondary
particles;\cite{Murase_2023} these interactions produce copious pions, whose
decays subsequently generate high-energy neutrinos and $\gamma$-rays.
\cite{kelner2006energy} We consider the two primary production channels for
muon-flavored neutrinos, charged-pion decay and muon decay, and then the
visible decay of the HNL; polarization is tracked through all three stages.

Throughout this section, $E_N$ and $E$ denote laboratory-frame energies of
the HNL and of the electron, respectively, while $x$ and $y$ are the
corresponding dimensionless variables in the parent rest frame, defined
where they first appear. The helicity label $s=\pm1$ denotes the HNL spin
projection along its momentum, with $s=+1$ for a right-handed HNL; the same
convention is used in all three stages below.

\subsection{Production in pion decay}\label{sec:pion_decay}

The decay $\pi^+ \to \mu^+ + N$ is the HNL analogue of the
process $\pi^+ \to \mu^+ + \nu_\mu$.\cite{PhysRevD.24.1232}

By adapting the standard pion-decay calculation, we find that the decay width
to HNL is suppressed by the mixing angle and modified by a kinematic factor:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\pi \to \mu N}}{\Gamma_{\pi \to \mu \nu}} =
  |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times \rho_\pi (m_N) \label{eq:pion_HNL_rho}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
   & \rho_\pi (m_N) = \frac{(m_\pi^2 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}}{m_\mu^2 (m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2)^2} \label{eq:rho_pi} \\
   & \lambda(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 2xy - 2yz - 2zx
\end{align}
The function $\rho_\pi$ encodes the phase-space suppression.

The HNL polarization is important because it controls the angular
dependence of the subsequent decay. In the pion rest frame we have
\begin{equation}
  % \Gamma_{\pi \to N}^{(s)} \propto (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) m_\pi^2 - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2 - s \cdot (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}
  {\Gamma_{(s)}} \sim {(m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) m_\pi^2 - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2 - s(m_\mu^2 - m_N^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}} \label{eq:hnl_hel_pion}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the polarization of HNL, and $s=+1$ for right-handed polarized HNLs.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centerline{\includegraphics{figures/pion_decay_hel_br.pdf}}
  \caption{Eq.~(\ref{eq:hnl_hel_pion}) for different $s$. In pion decay,
  right-handed HNLs are more likely to be produced close to the phase
  space boundary.}
  \label{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist}
  \alttext{$\cos\theta_r$ when calculating HNL spectrum from pion decay.}
\end{figure}

Since the parent pion is a spin-zero scalar, the decay is isotropic 
in its rest frame. However, the polarization of the massive HNL needs to be 
carefully tracked, as the subsequent Lorentz boost to the laboratory 
frame induces a mass-dependent polarization redistribution.
% There are no differences in angular dependence for different helicity end
% states, since pions are unpolarized. After boosting to the lab frame,
% we must account for the polarization change on the HNL particles,
For a pion spectrum $J(E_\pi)$, we get
\begin{equation}
  % Q_{N}^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\frac{E_N}{\lambda^+}}^{\frac{E_N}{\lambda^-}} J(E_\pi) |U_{\mu N}|^2 \rho_\pi \times r_{(s)}(E_\pi, E_N) \times \frac{d E_\pi}{\lambda_{max} E_\pi} \label{eq:hnl_spec_from_pion}
  Q_{N}^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\lambda^-}^{\lambda^+} J(\frac{E_N}{x}) |U_{\mu N}|^2 \rho_\pi \times r_{(s)}(x, E_N) \times \frac{d x}{(\lambda^+ - \lambda^-) \cdot x} \label{eq:hnl_spec_from_pion}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
   & \lambda^{\pm}       = \frac{m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2 + m_N^2 \pm \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}}{2 m_\pi^2}       \\
   & \cos\theta_r        = \frac{x \, (m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) - 2 m_N^2}{x \, \sqrt{(1 - \frac{m_N^2}{E_N^2}) \, \lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}} \label{eq:cos_theta_r_pion}\\
   & r_{(s)}(x, E_N) = \frac{\Gamma_{(s)}\frac{1 + \cos\theta_r}{2} + \Gamma_{(-s)}\frac{1 - \cos\theta_r}{2}}{\Gamma_{(s)} + \Gamma_{(-s)}}
\end{align}
Here we assume $E_\pi \gg m_\pi$ to simplify the boost process.
This resembles the SM neutrino case\cite{kelner2006energy}, with
modifications arising from HNL mass and helicity separation.

  helicity flip will happen during Lorentz boost. When $m_N$
  is small $\cos\theta_r \approx 1$, combined with Fig.~(\ref{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist}),
  HNLs produced are almost left-handed; as $m_N$

\subsection{Production in muon decay}\label{sec:muon_decay}

Muons can decay into HNLs via a process analogous to their standard decay
into $\nu_\mu$. The decay width of $\mu^- \to N e^- \bar{\nu}_e$ has the
same form as that of the previously studied $\tau$ decay process
\cite{Bondarenko_2018}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\mu \to N}}{\Gamma_{\mu \to \nu_\mu}} = |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times \rho_\mu(m_N)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\mu(m_N) = - r_N^8 + 8 r_N^6 - 24 r_N^4 \ln(r_N) - 8 r_N^2 + 1 \label{eq:rho_mu},\;\;
  r_N = m_N / m_\mu
\end{equation}
The helicity-dependent spectrum of the HNL in the muon rest frame is given by
\begin{align}
  \frac{d\Gamma}{dE} = \frac{G_F^2}{24 \pi^3} |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times |\mathbf{p}| \cdot [ & 3 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) E - 4 m_\mu E^2 - 2 m_N^2 m_\mu \nonumber                   \\
                                                                                    & - s \cdot |\mathbf{p}| (m_N^2 + 3 m_\mu^2 - 4 m_\mu E) ] \label{eq:mu_HNL_spec}
\end{align}
Several examples are plotted in Fig.~(\ref{fig:muon_decay_spec}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centerline{\includegraphics{figures/muon_decay_spectrum.pdf}}
  \caption{Energy spectrum of HNL when muons decay into HNLs in the muon rest
  frame. The results are normalized to the $m_N = 0$ case, which corresponds
  to the SM neutrino and has no right-handed component. As $m_N$ increases,
  the shape of the spectrum remains roughly the same, but the maximum energy
  increases while the total decay width decreases, yielding a increasing
  fraction of right-handed HNLs.}
  \label{fig:muon_decay_spec}
  \alttext{HNL Spectrum of Muon Decay Separated by Helicity}
\end{figure}

A fully spin-correlated treatment could retain the polarization 
of the parent muons and the angular relation between their 
spin and decay products. Here we neglect parent-muon polarization
as a simplifying approximation. This approximation isolates 
the HNL mass and polarization dependence but should not be
interpreted as a demonstrated consequence of complete magnetic 
depolarization in the source. For a given muon spectrum, we have
\begin{align}
  Q_N^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}^{1} & \frac{d y}{2y} \, Q_\mu(\frac{E_N}{y}) |U_{\mu N}|^2 \rho_\mu \nonumber \cdot \int_{x^-}^{x^+}  \frac{d x}{\sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}} \nonumber \\
  & \cdot \left(\frac{1 + \cos\theta_r}{2} g_N^{(s)}(x) + \frac{1 - \cos\theta_r}{2} g_N^{(-s)}(x)\right) \label{eq:muon_lab_HNL_spec}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  &x^+ = \frac{m_N^2 + m_\mu^2}{2 m_\mu^2}, \; x^- = \frac{1}{2} \left( y + \frac{1}{y} \cdot \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}\right) \\
  &\cos\theta_0 = \frac{y - x}{\sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}} \\
  &\cos\theta_r = \frac{1}{y \, \sqrt{1 - \frac{m_N^2}{E_N^2}}}\left( \sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}} + x \cos\theta_0\right)
\end{align}
where $g^{(s)}_N(x)$ is normalized as
\begin{equation}
  \int^{x^+}_{\frac{m_N}{m_\mu}} dx \sum_{s=\pm 1} g^{(s)}_N(x) = 1
\end{equation}

\subsection{Visible three-body decay}\label{sec:visible_decay}

Kinematically, low-mass HNLs in the ISS model can decay only into leptons.
The possible final states are $\nu_\mu e^- e^+$ and
$\nu_\mu \nu_l \bar{\nu}_l$. These processes have been well
studied\cite{Bondarenko_2018, Atre_2009, Gorbunov_2007};
their decay widths are given by
\begin{align}
  \Gamma_{N \to 3\nu}          & = \frac{G_F^2 m_N^5}{192 \pi^3} \times |U_{\mu N}|^2 \label{eq:HNL_3nu_decay}               \\
  \Gamma_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} & = \frac{G_F^2 m_N^5}{192 \pi^3} \times |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times C_1^e \label{eq:HNL_nuee_decay}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
  C_1^e = \frac{1}{4}\left(1 - 4 \sin^2 \theta_W + 8 \sin^4 \theta_W\right)
\end{equation}
To simplify the problem, we neglect the electron mass. The $3\nu$ final state
includes all neutrino flavor combinations.

An HNL produced in the central region can affect the 
electromagnetic signal only if its energy is ultimately 
transferred to visible charged particles, thus the branching 
ratio for an HNL to undergo a visible decay is
\begin{equation}
  BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} = \frac{C_1^e}{1 + C_1^e} \approx 0.11
\end{equation}
This implies a substantial energy loss, since only about one-tenth of the
HNL decays produce charged leptons.

The angular and energy distributions of electrons/positrons are
\begin{align}
  g_e^{(1)}(x, \cos{\theta}) = & \frac{8}{C_1^e} \cdot x^2 \cdot \{ [(4 - 16 s_W^2 + 64 s_W^4) x - (1 - 4 s_W^2 + 28 s_W^4)] \cos{\theta} \nonumber   \\
                               & - (4 - 16 s_W^2 + 64 s_W^4) x + (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 36 s_W^4) \} \label{eq:g_e_p2}                                       \\
  g_e^{(2)}(x, \cos{\theta}) = & \frac{16}{C_1^e} \cdot x^2 \cdot \{ [(6 - 24 s_W^2 + 32 s_W^4) x - (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 14 s_W^4)] \cos{\theta} \nonumber \\
                               & - (6 - 24 s_W^2 + 32 s_W^4) x + (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 18 s_W^4) \} \label{eq:g_e_p3}
\end{align}
Here, $g_e^{(1)}$ and $g_e^{(2)}$ represent the differential 
distributions for the outgoing electron ($e^-$) and positron 
($e^+$), respectively, with $x = E_e / m_N$ and $s_W = \sin{\theta_W}$. 
The angle $\theta$ is defined between the outgoing lepton momentum and the parent HNL spin. 
To obtain the total injected lepton spectrum, we sum over both $e^-$ and $e^+$:
\begin{align}
  Q_e^{(Lab)}(E) = BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} \cdot \int_{E}^{+\infty} &d E_N \, Q_N^{(s)}(E_N) \nonumber \\
  &\cdot \int_{\frac{E}{2 E_N}}^{\frac{1}{2}}  \frac{d x}{x E_N} \, \sum_{i=1,2} g_e^{(i)}(x, s (\frac{E}{x E_N} - 1))
\end{align}
Evaluating the angular and energy integrals yields
\begin{align}
  Q_e^{(Lab)}(E) = &BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}}  \nonumber\\
                   &\cdot \int_{0}^{1} \frac{d y}{3y} \, Q_N^{(s)}(\frac{E}{y}) [16 y^3 - 27 y^2 + 11 -  s \cdot (32 y^3 - 63 y^2 + 36 y - 5) ]
\end{align}
where $y = E / E_N$. The dependence on the Weinberg angle $\theta_W$
cancels in the sum: in the massless limit $m_e \to 0$, helicity-flipping
mass terms vanish, and the vector--axial-vector ($V$--$A$) interference
terms are odd under charge conjugation ($e^- \leftrightarrow e^+$) and
therefore cancel between the electron and positron yields, leaving the total
spectral shape independent of $\sin^2\theta_W$.


\section{HNL in NGC 1068}\label{sec:HNLs_in_NGC_1068}
The observed neutrino flux of NGC 1068 is commonly modeled as originating
from charged-pion decay \cite{murase2020hidden}. Combining the analytical
neutrino spectrum from pion decays \cite{kelner2006energy} with theoretical
models of NGC 1068 \cite{murase2020hidden}, we adopt the following
parameterization for the pion spectrum:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{\pi^\pm}(E) = \Phi_0 \cdot \left( \frac{E}{\qty{1}{GeV}}\right)^{-\Gamma} \cdot e^{-(E / E_c)^\beta} \label{eq:pion_spec_form}
\end{equation}
with best-fit values
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_0 = \qty{4.7e-12}{GeV^{-1}.cm^{-2}.s^{-1}}, \Gamma = 0.1, E_c = \qty{6.2}{GeV} , \beta=0.3 \label{eq:pion_spec_par}
\end{equation}
visualized in
visualized in Fig.~(\ref{fig:pion_spec_fit_show}). The parameters are
obtained by fitting this form to the theoretical neutrino flux of
Ref.~\cite{murase2020hidden}, since the uncertainties and limited energy
range of the IceCube data do not by themselves determine the pion spectrum.
This spectrum provides the parent-particle input for the production
calculation of Sec.~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay}.

\subsection{Decay length and the escape assumption}\label{sec:decay_length}

For HNLs to mediate energy transport from the opaque core to a transparent
external environment, their decay length must be sufficiently large. We have
\begin{equation}
  l_{dec} = \frac{E_N}{m_N} \, c \, \frac{\hbar}{\Gamma_{tot}} = \frac{192 \pi^3 c \hbar}{G_F^2(1 + C_1^e)} \cdot \frac{E_N}{m_N^6 |U_{\mu N}|^2}
\end{equation}
For example, setting $|U_{\mu N}|^2=10^{-5}$, $m_N=\qty{20}{MeV}$, and
$E_N=\qty{1}{TeV}$ yields $l_{dec}\approx\qty{0.4}{pc}$, which implies that
HNLs are unlikely to escape into interstellar space, while some decays will
still occur inside the optically opaque region.

Because $l_{dec}\propto E_N/(m_N^6|U_{\mu N}|^2)$, the decay length is extremely
sensitive to $m_N$: over the \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range it varies by more
than two orders of magnitude.
Since $l_{dec}\propto1/|U_{\mu N}|^2$, at the experimental bound
$|U_{\mu N}|^2\lesssim10^{-4}$ (the representative bound over the
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range) the mixing is one order of magnitude larger
than the value used above, so that $l_{dec}$ is correspondingly shorter by
about one order of magnitude (approximately \qty{0.04}{pc}). Most HNLs then can
decay inside the opaque core and their visible products are absorbed before
escape. We
therefore adopt the optimistic assumption that all HNLs decay beyond the
core and that their energy escapes unattenuated; this assumption maximizes
the predicted flux and makes the resulting exclusion conservative.

\subsection{Comparison with observations and the suppression budget}
\label{sec:comparison}

Setting $|U_{\mu N}|^2 = 1$, the predicted photon flux lies three to four
orders of magnitude below the observational upper limits. The residual
suppression arises mainly from the small visible branching ratio
$BR\simeq0.11$, which contributes about one order of magnitude, and from the
fraction of the parent pion or muon energy carried into the visible channel
together with the radiation efficiency of the adopted environment, which
account for the remaining orders. The phase-space factors $\rho_\pi,\rho_\mu$ are of order
unity over most of the \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range and differ substantially
from unity only near the kinematic endpoint, so they are not counted as a
source of orders of magnitude. Applying the experimental bound
$|U_{\mu N}|^2 \lesssim10^{-4}$ (the representative value over this range)
adds four more orders of magnitude, bringing the total suppression to seven
to eight orders.

Two qualifications do not alter this result. First, the experimental
bound on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ is mass dependent and changing over the
entire \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range, although the difference is minor.
Second, an accurate flux from a finite external region must account for the
probability of decay within that region; the present treatment instead
assumes that all HNLs deposit their visible products in the chosen zone,
which makes the estimate optimistic rather than fully propagated. The first
qualification affects the bound only at the same order, and the second can
only reduce the predicted flux, so the exclusion is conservative. The
comparison in Fig.~(\ref{fig:gamma_show_compare}) uses the Fermi-LAT
point-source upper limits.\cite{Acciari_2019} Within the range
$m_N\simeq\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV}$, the channel is therefore excluded as an
explanation of the $\gamma$-ray excess, although the laboratory limits on
$|U_{\mu N}|^2$ are unchanged.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
We have presented a polarization-resolved calculation of HNL production and
decay in the muon-neutrino sector and applied it to the multi-messenger
observations of NGC 1068. The HNL spectra from
$\pi^\pm\to N+\mu^\pm$ and $\mu^\pm\to N+e^\pm+\bar{\nu}_e$ were computed
separately for the two polarization states, boosted to the laboratory frame with
the mass-dependent polarization redistribution retained, and converted into the
electron/positron spectrum of the visible decay
$N\to\nu_\mu+e^+e^-$. The resulting $\gamma$-ray emission, evaluated for a
representative environment of the source, lies at least \numrange{7}{8}
orders of magnitude below the observational limits once the laboratory
constraint on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ is imposed, and remains \numrange{3}{4} orders
of magnitude below them even for $|U_{\mu N}|^2=1$. The weakly coupled HNL
channel is therefore excluded as the origin of the $\gamma$-ray excess. The
suppression factorizes approximately into the visible branching ratio, the
fraction of the parent energy transferred to the electron--positron pair,
the radiative efficiency of the environment, and the mixing-angle
constraint (Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}).

The approximations adopted here are conservative---in particular, all HNLs
are assumed to decay within the adopted emission region---so a more complete
treatment of transport can only reduce the predicted flux. The mass
dependence of the laboratory bound on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ over the
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range is a minor residual uncertainty
(Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}). The present analysis does not modify the
laboratory limits on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$, but it converts the multi-messenger
data of NGC 1068 into an exclusionary constraint on this HNL channel that is
complementary to laboratory searches.

The negative result is itself informative. It excludes a well-motivated
weakly coupled transport channel for the excess and shifts the emphasis to
alternatives, most notably a refined description of the AGN environment
underlying current electromagnetic cascade models,\cite{murase2020hidden}
or energy transport mediated by other weakly interacting particles. The
polarization-resolved framework is not specific to this source or mass
window: it applies to any hadronic source in which HNLs are produced, to
other HNL masses, and to other neutral particles whose visible decay
products retain polarization information.

Finally, the helicity-tracking method is directly relevant to
accelerator-based HNL analyses. Polarization effects have already been
included in accelerator simulations for the DUNE near
detector.\cite{Ballett_2020} Polarization-aware decay distributions could
similarly be incorporated into the displaced-vertex and track reconstruction
of SHiP\cite{shipcollaboration2019sensitivityshipexperimentheavy}, the LArTPC mass reconstruction\cite{Alves_2025}, 
and the associated acceptance and likelihood
calculations. Such inputs could also be used to reinterpret null searches
and optimize detector geometries.
