\begin{abstract}
The active galactic nucleus NGC 1068, recently identified as a high-energy
neutrino source by IceCube, exhibits a GeV $\gamma$-ray flux that exceeds
predictions from opaque corona cascade models. This tension suggests the
existence of non-attenuated energy transport mechanisms from the compact
core. We investigate whether heavy neutral leptons (HNLs), coupled to the
Standard Model through mixing with muon neutrinos, can provide such a
channel, and we derive the resulting \emph{exclusionary constraint} on the
HNL parameter space from the multi-messenger observations of NGC 1068. We
calculate the production of HNLs in charged-pion and muon decays associated
with hadronic interactions, explicitly tracking the mass-dependent
polarization redistribution. We then derive the spectrum from the visible
$N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$ decay channel and evaluate its secondary
electromagnetic emission. We find that the predicted photon flux is at least
\numrange{7}{8} orders of magnitude below the observational upper limits
once experimental bounds on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ are applied, which decisively excludes
this HNL channel as the origin of the $\gamma$-ray excess. Even under an
optimistic estimate in which particle transportation is neglected, the
conclusion remains unchanged, and the exclusion of this channel is
correspondingly strengthened. Nevertheless, the polarization-resolved
framework developed here remains applicable to broader studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Multi-messenger astronomy has turned astrophysical accelerators into
laboratories for physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM). The IceCube
collaboration has identified the active galactic nucleus (AGN) NGC 1068 as a
point source of neutrinos at energies around \qty{1}{TeV},\cite{icecube2022evidence} and the KM3NeT/ARCA telescope has reported a
high-energy event candidate possibly reaching \qty{220}{PeV}.\cite{km3net2025observation} These observations establish that hadronic
acceleration operates in at least some AGN cores, and they fix the neutrino
flux that any transport mechanism in such a source must reproduce.

% Numerous terrestrial experiments are dedicated to searching for signals of BSM physics.\cite{PhysRevLett.103.241802}

The same source is anomalous in $\gamma$-rays. The GeV $\gamma$-ray flux of
NGC 1068 measured by Fermi-LAT exceeds the predictions of opaque corona
cascade models, in which the $\gamma$ rays are generated in the optically
thick core and attenuated before escape.\cite{Acciari_2019,murase2020hidden}
The excess therefore points to a transport channel that
carries energy out of the compact core without being absorbed, and that
converts this energy into observable photons only beyond the opaque region.
Neutral, weakly interacting particles are natural candidates for such a
channel: they escape the core freely and decay or scatter in the surrounding
environment, where their secondaries radiate. The question we address is
whether a specific BSM particle can play this role.

HNLs are motivated here on two levels: theoretically, they are among the
simplest BSM particles that generate neutrino mass; phenomenologically, an
MeV-scale HNL mixed with the muon neutrino is produced in the dominant
neutrino production chain of NGC 1068 (pion and muon decays) and possesses
a visible decay channel, so that it is a qualified candidate for the
transport channel of this source. The non-zero but extremely small active
neutrino masses require physics beyond the Standard Model,
\cite{ASAKA200517, Boyarsky_2019} and HNLs are among the simplest mediators
of neutrino mass, simultaneously providing a dark-matter candidate and a
target for laboratory searches.\cite{PhysRevLett.103.241802} In the Inverse
Seesaw (ISS) mechanism the smallness of the light neutrino mass is
controlled by a small lepton-number-violating parameter rather than by a
very large Majorana mass, so that the mixing with active neutrinos can
remain large enough to be phenomenologically relevant. Terrestrial
experiments have not yet observed a definite HNL signal, but they constrain
the mixing with the muon neutrino to $|U_{\mu N}|^2 \lesssim 10^{-4}$ for
\qty{3}{MeV} $< m_N <$ \qty{19.5}{MeV};\cite{bryman2019constraints} the
bound is slightly weaker but of the same order over the
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range considered here. These theoretical and
phenomenological conditions are both met in this source, which is why we
single out HNLs rather than an arbitrary BSM particle; other neutral
candidates, such as axion-like particles, have been studied separately for
NGC 1068,\cite{pant2024constraining} and we do not attempt a comparative
survey here.

This motivates a dedicated calculation. A precise statement cannot rely on
the massless neutrino results: for $m_N$ in the \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV}
range the HNL is non-relativistic in the parent rest frame, phase space is
strongly suppressed, and the helicity produced in the weak decay is
redistributed by the boost in a mass-dependent way. This is visible already
at the production stage: as shown in
Figs.~(\ref{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist})--(\ref{fig:muon_decay_spec}) below,
right-handed HNLs, which are absent in the massless limit, become an
appreciable fraction of the pion-decay yield as $m_N$ approaches the
phase-space boundary, and Fig.~(\ref{fig:muon_decay_spec}) shows the same
effect in muon decay. These polarization states are not washed out in the
subsequent decay: the visible channel $N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$ inherits
the helicity through the angular distributions $g_e^{(i)}(x,\cos\theta)$ of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:g_e_p2}--\eqref{eq:g_e_p3}, so the electron spectrum, and
hence the $\gamma$-ray yield, depends on the polarization of the parent
HNL. % 【Fix 6】
A polarization-resolved treatment is therefore adopted here to convert the
NGC 1068 multi-messenger data into a quantitative statement about this
channel, and it is also what makes the framework reusable for other sources
and other HNL mass windows.

In this paper we test whether this HNL channel can account for the
$\gamma$-ray excess of NGC 1068, and we derive the corresponding
\emph{exclusionary constraint} on the HNL parameter space. We compute the
HNL source spectrum from $\pi^\pm \to \mu^\pm + N$ and
$\mu^\pm \to N + e^\pm + \bar{\nu}_e$, separating the two helicity states
and boosting to the laboratory frame while tracking the polarization
redistribution. We then derive the electron/positron spectrum from the
visible decay $N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$, propagate these leptons in a
representative environment of NGC 1068, and compute the resulting
electromagnetic emission with GAMERA \cite{Hahn:2016CO}. Comparing with the
observed $\gamma$-ray flux, we find that the predicted photon flux lies at
least \numrange{7}{8} orders of magnitude below the observational upper
limits once the experimental bound on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ is applied. Even under an
optimistic estimate in which particle transport is neglected, the
conclusion is unchanged. The HNL channel is therefore decisively excluded as
the origin of the $\gamma$-ray excess, and the exclusionary constraint on
this channel is correspondingly strong.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:iss_model} gives the HNL
coupling, the ISS parameter range, the single-Dirac approximation, and the
decay channels. Section~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay} presents the
polarization-resolved calculations of HNL production in pion and muon decays
and of the visible three-body decay $N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$, and
collects them into the electron source function injected into the radiation
calculation. Section~\ref{sec:HNLs_in_NGC_1068} applies this source function
to the environment of NGC 1068, compares with observations, and gives the
suppression budget and its robustness. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions}
summarizes our conclusions.

\section{ISS Model: Parameters and Prerequisites}\label{sec:iss_model}

This section provides the four ingredients needed below: the coupling of
HNLs to the SM, the allowed ranges of $m_N$ and $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ in the ISS, the
justification of the single-Dirac approximation, and the visible decay
channel with its branching ratio. The production and decay calculations of
Sec.~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay} and the experimental bound and
suppression budget of Sec.~\ref{sec:HNLs_in_NGC_1068} rest on them.

\subsection{Coupling}

HNLs interact with the SM only through their mixing with the active
neutrinos. Therefore any process that produces $\nu_\mu$ also produces $N$,
with a rate multiplied by $|U_{\mu N}|^2$. Section~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay}
uses this to compute $N$ production in charged-pion and muon decay chains.

\subsection{ISS and parameter ranges}

In the Type-I seesaw:\cite{mohapatra1980neutrino,minkowski1977mu,yanagida1980horizontal,gellmann2013complexspinorsunifiedtheories,glashow1980future,Casas_2001}
\begin{equation}
  m_\nu = \frac{m_D^2}{M_R}, \; m_N = M_R, \; |U|^2 = \left( \frac{m_D}{M_R} \right)^2
\end{equation}
For an
observable $M_R$ the mixing is usually too small to be probed. The Inverse
Seesaw (ISS) \cite{mohapatra1980neutrino,minkowski1977mu,yanagida1980horizontal,gellmann2013complexspinorsunifiedtheories,glashow1980future,Casas_2001}
introduces another fermion field $S$, and the mass matrix reads
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \overline{\nu_L} & \overline{\nu_R^c} & \overline{S^c}
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0   & m_D & 0   \\
    m_D & 0   & M   \\
    0   & M   & \mu
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_L^c \\
    \nu_R   \\
    S
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is a small lepton-number-violating mass term.
For $\mu\ll M$ and $\mu\ll m_D$,
\begin{equation}
  m_\nu = \mu \frac{m_D^2}{M^2}, \; m_N = M, \; |U|^2 = \frac{m_\nu}{\mu}
\end{equation}
The key point is that the smallness of the light neutrino mass is controlled
by the small parameter $\mu$ rather than by a very large $M$, so that for
comparable neutrino masses the mixing can be larger than in the Type-I case.

For the parameters used here, $|U|^2$ can reach the $10^{-4}$ level: taking
$m_\nu\sim0.05$~eV and $\mu\sim\mathrm{keV}$ gives
$|U|^2=m_\nu/\mu\sim10^{-4}$, while $\mu\ll m_D,M$ is satisfied. This is the
same order as the experimental bound quoted in Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}, and it
is what allows Sec.~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay} to take $|U|^2$ not small
and Sec.~\ref{sec:HNLs_in_NGC_1068} to use the bound
$|U_{\mu N}|^2 \lesssim 10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Single-Dirac approximation}

For small $\mu$ the heavy states form a nearly degenerate pseudo-Dirac pair.\cite{Das_2013,Deppisch_2015}
Over the range $m_N \simeq \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV}$ and $\mu \ll m_D,M$
considered here, the mass splitting is much smaller than $m_N$, so that
production and decay cannot resolve the two states; summing over helicities
is therefore equivalent to a single Dirac fermion. The helicity-resolved
calculation of Sec.~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay}, with $s = \pm 1$, rests on
this approximation.

\subsection{Decay channels and visible branching ratio}

Because the HNLs considered here are lighter than the charged-pion decay
threshold, their production and decay are governed by low-energy weak
processes. They decay only into leptons: a three-neutrino channel
(invisible) and a $\nu_\mu e^+e^-$ channel (visible).
% with a visible branching ratio of order $0.1$. 
Section~\ref{sec:visible_decay} gives the
explicit value $BR \simeq 0.11$, which is the first entry of the suppression
budget in Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison} and one of the three sources discussed
in Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusions}.

\section{Polarization-Resolved HNL Production and Decay}
\label{sec:HNL_production_decay}

HNLs interact only through their mixing with SM neutrinos, which implies
that processes producing SM neutrinos will also produce HNLs, provided they
are kinematically allowed. Under AGN conditions, high-energy cosmic rays
undergo $p-p$ and $p-\gamma$ interactions, which are thought to be the
dominant sources of secondary particles;\cite{Murase_2023} those processes
create a large number of pions, whose decays subsequently produce
high-energy cosmic rays, including neutrinos and $\gamma$-rays.
\cite{kelner2006energy} We consider the two production channels most
directly related to the muon-neutrino sector, charged pion decay and muon
decay, and then the visible decay of the HNL; polarization is tracked
through all three stages.

Throughout this section, $E_N$ and $E$ denote laboratory-frame energies of
the HNL and of the electron, respectively, while $x$ and $y$ are the
corresponding dimensionless variables in the parent rest frame, defined
where they first appear. The helicity label $s=\pm1$ denotes the HNL spin
projection along its momentum, with $s=+1$ for a right-handed HNL; the same
convention is used in all three stages below.

\subsection{Production in pion decay}\label{sec:pion_decay}

The decay $\pi^+ \to \mu^+ + N$ is the HNL analogue of the
process $\pi^+ \to \mu^+ + \nu_\mu$.\cite{PhysRevD.24.1232}

By slightly modifying the process of SM pion decay, we find that the decay width
to HNL is suppressed by the mixing angle and modified by a kinematic factor:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\pi \to \mu N}}{\Gamma_{\pi \to \mu \nu}} =
  |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times \rho_\pi (m_N) \label{eq:pion_HNL_rho}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
   & \rho_\pi (m_N) = \frac{(m_\pi^2 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}}{m_\mu^2 (m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2)^2} \label{eq:rho_pi} \\
   & \lambda(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 2xy - 2yz - 2zx
\end{align}
The function $\rho_\pi$ encodes the phase-space suppression.

It is useful to determine the polarization of the HNLs, since
it will play a role in the angular dependence when working on the
decay of HNL. In the pion rest frame we have
\begin{equation}
  % \Gamma_{\pi \to N}^{(s)} \propto (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) m_\pi^2 - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2 - s \cdot (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}
  {\Gamma_{(s)}} \sim {(m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) m_\pi^2 - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2 - s(m_\mu^2 - m_N^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}} \label{eq:hnl_hel_pion}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the polarization of HNL, and $s=+1$ for right-handed polarized HNLs.

  \centerline{\includegraphics{figures/pion_decay_hel_br.pdf}}
  \caption{Eq.~(\ref{eq:hnl_hel_pion}) for different $s$. In pion decay,
  right-handed HNLs are more likely to be produced close to the phase
  space boundary.}
  \label{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist}
  \alttext{$\cos\theta_r$ when calculating HNL spectrum from pion decay.}
\end{figure}

Since the parent pion is a spin-zero scalar, the decay is isotropic 
in its rest frame. However, the polarization of the massive HNL needs to be 
carefully tracked, as the subsequent Lorentz boost to the laboratory 
frame induces a mass-dependent polarization redistribution.
% There are no differences in angular dependence for different helicity end
% states, since pions are unpolarized. After boosting to the lab frame,
% we must account for the polarization change on the HNL particles,
For a pion spectrum $J(E_\pi)$, we get
\begin{equation}
  % Q_{N}^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\frac{E_N}{\lambda^+}}^{\frac{E_N}{\lambda^-}} J(E_\pi) |U_{\mu N}|^2 \rho_\pi \times r_{(s)}(E_\pi, E_N) \times \frac{d E_\pi}{\lambda_{max} E_\pi} \label{eq:hnl_spec_from_pion}
  Q_{N}^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\lambda^-}^{\lambda^+} J(\frac{E_N}{x}) |U_{\mu N}|^2 \rho_\pi \times r_{(s)}(x, E_N) \times \frac{d x}{(\lambda^+ - \lambda^-) \cdot x} \label{eq:hnl_spec_from_pion}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
   & \lambda^{\pm}       = \frac{m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2 + m_N^2 \pm \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}}{2 m_\pi^2}       \\
   & \cos\theta_r        = \frac{x \, (m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) - 2 m_N^2}{x \, \sqrt{(1 - \frac{m_N^2}{E_N^2}) \, \lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}} \label{eq:cos_theta_r_pion}\\
   & r_{(s)}(x, E_N) = \frac{\Gamma_{(s)}\frac{1 + \cos\theta_r}{2} + \Gamma_{(-s)}\frac{1 - \cos\theta_r}{2}}{\Gamma_{(s)} + \Gamma_{(-s)}}
\end{align}
Here we assume $E_\pi \gg m_\pi$ to simplify the boost process.
This resembles the SM neutrino case\cite{kelner2006energy}, with
modifications arising from HNL mass and helicity separation.

  is small $\cos\theta_r \approx 1$, combined with Fig.~(\ref{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist}),
  HNLs produced are almost left-handed; as $m_N$

\subsection{Production in muon decay}\label{sec:muon_decay}

Muons can decay into HNLs via a process analogous to 
their standard decay into $\nu_\mu$. We find the decay width of $\mu^- \to N e^- \bar{\nu}_e$ has the same
form as the previously studied $\tau$ decay process
\cite{Bondarenko_2018}, since this process is symmetric between
$\mu$ and $\tau$, which is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\mu \to N}}{\Gamma_{\mu \to \nu_\mu}} = |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times \rho_\mu(m_N)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\mu(m_N) = - r_N^8 + 8 r_N^6 - 24 r_N^4 \ln(r_N) - 8 r_N^2 + 1 \label{eq:rho_mu},\;\;
  r_N = m_N / m_\mu
\end{equation}
The helicity-dependent spectrum of the HNL in the muon rest frame is given by
\begin{align}
  \frac{d\Gamma}{dE} = \frac{G_F^2}{24 \pi^3} |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times |\mathbf{p}| \cdot [ & 3 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) E - 4 m_\mu E^2 - 2 m_N^2 m_\mu \nonumber                   \\
                                                                                    & - s \cdot |\mathbf{p}| (m_N^2 + 3 m_\mu^2 - 4 m_\mu E) ] \label{eq:mu_HNL_spec}
\end{align}
Several examples are plotted in Fig.~(\ref{fig:muon_decay_spec}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centerline{\includegraphics{figures/muon_decay_spectrum.pdf}}
  \caption{Energy spectrum of HNL when muons decay into HNLs in muon rest frame.
  Normalized under $m_N = 0$, which
  basically resembles SM neutrino, with no right-handed product produced.
  When $m_N$ increases, the shape of the spectrum remains roughly the same, 
  but the maximum energy increases while the total decay
  width decreases, yielding a non-negligible fraction of right-handed HNLs.}
  \label{fig:muon_decay_spec}
  \alttext{HNL Spectrum of Muon Decay Separated by Helicity}
\end{figure}

% Although one could retain the muon polarization and compute the angular
% dependence of the resulting HNL helicity states, we neglect muon
% polarization here under the assumption that the turbulent magnetic
% fields in the AGN completely depolarize the muons, in favor of simplifying
% our calculation.

% For different HNL energy, we have
% \begin{align}
%   \frac{d\Gamma_{(s)}}{dE} \sim \; &3 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) E - 4 m_\mu E^2 - 2m_N^2 m_\mu \nonumber \\
%   &- s \cdot |\mathbf{p}| (m_N^2 + 3 m_\mu^2 - 4 m_\mu E) 
% \end{align}

A fully spin-correlated treatment could retain the polarization 
of the parent muons and the angular relation between their 
spin and decay products. Here we neglect parent-muon polarization
as a simplifying approximation. This approximation isolates 
the HNL mass and polarization dependence but should not be
interpreted as a demonstrated consequence of complete magnetic 
depolarization in the source. For a given muon spectrum, we have
\begin{align}
  Q_N^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}^{1} & \frac{d y}{2y} \, Q_\mu(\frac{E_N}{y}) |U_{\mu N}|^2 \rho_\mu \nonumber \cdot \int_{x^-}^{x^+}  \frac{d x}{\sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}} \nonumber \\
  & \cdot \left(\frac{1 + \cos\theta_r}{2} g_N^{(s)}(x) + \frac{1 - \cos\theta_r}{2} g_N^{(-s)}(x)\right) \label{eq:muon_lab_HNL_spec}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  &x^+ = \frac{m_N^2 + m_\mu^2}{2 m_\mu^2}, \; x^- = \frac{1}{2} \left( y + \frac{1}{y} \cdot \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}\right) \\
  &\cos\theta_0 = \frac{y - x}{\sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}} \\
  &\cos\theta_r = \frac{1}{y \, \sqrt{1 - \frac{m_N^2}{E_N^2}}}\left( \sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}} + x \cos\theta_0\right)
\end{align}
where $g^{(s)}_N(x)$ is normalized as
\begin{equation}
  \int^{x^+}_{\frac{m_N}{m_\mu}} dx \sum_{s=\pm 1} g^{(s)}_N(x) = 1
\end{equation}

\subsection{Visible three-body decay}\label{sec:visible_decay}

Kinematically, low-mass HNLs in the ISS model will exclusively
decay into other leptons. Possible products are $\nu_\mu e^- e^+$
and $\nu_\mu \nu_l \bar{\nu}_l$. These processes have been well studied\cite{Bondarenko_2018, Atre_2009, Gorbunov_2007};
their decay widths are given by
\begin{align}
  \Gamma_{N \to 3\nu}          & = \frac{G_F^2 m_N^5}{192 \pi^3} \times |U_{\mu N}|^2 \label{eq:HNL_3nu_decay}               \\
  \Gamma_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} & = \frac{G_F^2 m_N^5}{192 \pi^3} \times |U_{\mu N}|^2 \times C_1^e \label{eq:HNL_nuee_decay}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
  C_1^e = \frac{1}{4}\left(1 - 4 \sin^2 \theta_W + 8 \sin^4 \theta_W\right)
\end{equation}
To simplify the problem, we neglect the electron mass. The $3\nu$ final state
includes all neutrino flavor combinations.

An HNL produced in the central region can affect the 
electromagnetic signal only if its energy is ultimately 
transferred to visible charged particles, thus the branching 
ratio for an HNL to undergo a visible decay is
\begin{equation}
  BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} = \frac{C_1^e}{1 + C_1^e} \approx 0.11
\end{equation}
This results in substantial energy loss, as only approximately
one-tenth of the HNL decays are observable.

The angular and energy distributions of electrons/positrons are
\begin{align}
  g_e^{(1)}(x, \cos{\theta}) = & \frac{8}{C_1^e} \cdot x^2 \cdot \{ [(4 - 16 s_W^2 + 64 s_W^4) x - (1 - 4 s_W^2 + 28 s_W^4)] \cos{\theta} \nonumber   \\
                               & - (4 - 16 s_W^2 + 64 s_W^4) x + (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 36 s_W^4) \} \label{eq:g_e_p2}                                       \\
  g_e^{(2)}(x, \cos{\theta}) = & \frac{16}{C_1^e} \cdot x^2 \cdot \{ [(6 - 24 s_W^2 + 32 s_W^4) x - (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 14 s_W^4)] \cos{\theta} \nonumber \\
                               & - (6 - 24 s_W^2 + 32 s_W^4) x + (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 18 s_W^4) \} \label{eq:g_e_p3}
\end{align}
Here, $g_e^{(1)}$ and $g_e^{(2)}$ represent the differential 
distributions for the outgoing electron ($e^-$) and positron 
($e^+$), respectively, with $x = E_e / m_N$ and $s_W = \sin{\theta_W}$. 
The angle $\theta$ is defined between the outgoing lepton momentum and the parent HNL spin. 
To obtain the total injected lepton spectrum, we sum over both $e^-$ and $e^+$:
\begin{align}
  Q_e^{(Lab)}(E) = BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} \cdot \int_{E}^{+\infty} &d E_N \, Q_N^{(s)}(E_N) \nonumber \\
  &\cdot \int_{\frac{E}{2 E_N}}^{\frac{1}{2}}  \frac{d x}{x E_N} \, \sum_{i=1,2} g_e^{(i)}(x, s (\frac{E}{x E_N} - 1))
\end{align}
Evaluating the angular and energy integrals yields
\begin{align}
  Q_e^{(Lab)}(E) = &BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}}  \nonumber\\
                   &\cdot \int_{0}^{1} \frac{d y}{3y} \, Q_N^{(s)}(\frac{E}{y}) [16 y^3 - 27 y^2 + 11 -  s \cdot (32 y^3 - 63 y^2 + 36 y - 5) ]
\end{align}
where $y = E / E_N$. 
Notice that the dependence on the Weinberg angle $\theta_W$ 
cancels completely in the sum. In the massless limit $m_e \to 0$, 
helicity-flipping mass terms vanish, and the vector--axial-vector 
($V$-$A$) interference terms are odd under charge conjugation 
($e^- \leftrightarrow e^+$); they therefore cancel when summing 
over the electron and positron yields, leaving the total 
spectral shape independent of $\sin^2\theta_W$.


\section{HNL in NGC 1068}\label{sec:HNLs_in_NGC_1068}
According to IceCube observations, NGC 1068 has been identified as a
neutrino point source, and the dominant channel of neutrino production is
charged pion decay \cite{murase2020hidden}. By combining the analytical
neutrino spectrum from pion decays \cite{kelner2006energy} with theoretical
models of NGC 1068 \cite{murase2020hidden}, we adopt the following
parameterization as an approximation of the pion spectrum:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{\pi^\pm}(E) = \Phi_0 \cdot \left( \frac{E}{\qty{1}{GeV}}\right)^{-\Gamma} \cdot e^{-(E / E_c)^\beta} \label{eq:pion_spec_form}
\end{equation}
with best-fit values
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_0 = \qty{4.7e-12}{GeV^{-1}.cm^{-2}.s^{-1}}, \Gamma = 0.1, E_c = \qty{6.2}{GeV} , \beta=0.3 \label{eq:pion_spec_par}
\end{equation}
visualized in
Fig.~(\ref{fig:pion_spec_fit_show}). These parameters are obtained by fitting
this form to the neutrino flux of the theoretical model,\cite{murase2020hidden}
as the uncertainty and narrow energy band of observed flux of IceCube
makes it harder to produce a whole picture for parameters fitting. 
The spectrum provides the parent-particle
input for the production calculation of Sec.~\ref{sec:HNL_production_decay}.

\subsection{Decay length and the escape assumption}\label{sec:decay_length}

For HNLs to mediate energy transport from the opaque core to a transparent
external environment, their decay length must be sufficiently large. We have
\begin{equation}
  l_{dec} = \frac{E_N}{m_N} \, c \, \frac{\hbar}{\Gamma_{tot}} = \frac{192 \pi^3 c \hbar}{G_F^2(1 + C_1^e)} \cdot \frac{E_N}{m_N^6 |U_{\mu N}|^2}
\end{equation}
For demonstration, setting $|U_{\mu N}|^2=10^{-5}$, $m_N=\qty{20}{MeV}$, and
$E_N=\qty{1}{TeV}$ yields $l_{dec}\approx\qty{0.4}{pc}$, which implies that
HNLs are unlikely to escape into interstellar space, while some decays will
still occur inside the optically opaque region.

Because $l_{dec}\propto E_N/(m_N^6|U_{\mu N}|^2)$, the decay length is extremely
sensitive to $m_N$: over the \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range it varies by more
than two orders of magnitude. % 【Fix 3】
Since $l_{dec}\propto1/|U_{\mu N}|^2$, at the experimental bound
$|U_{\mu N}|^2\lesssim10^{-4}$ (the representative bound over the
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range) the mixing is one order of magnitude larger
than the value used above, so that $l_{dec}$ is correspondingly shorter by
about one order of magnitude (approximately \qty{0.04}{pc}). Most HNLs then
decay inside the opaque core and their visible products are absorbed before
escape. This further suppresses the signal and strengthens the exclusion. We
therefore adopt the optimistic assumption that all HNLs decay beyond the
core and that their energy escapes unattenuated; this assumption maximizes
the predicted flux and makes the resulting exclusion conservative.

\subsection{Comparison with observations and the suppression budget}
\label{sec:comparison}

Factoring out the mixing angle, i.e. setting $|U_{\mu N}|^2 = 1$, the predicted
photon flux lies three to four orders of magnitude below the observed
excess. This residual suppression arises mainly from two results already
obtained above: first, the small visible branching ratio $BR\simeq0.11$,
which contributes about one order of magnitude; second, the fact that the
HNL carries only a fraction of the parent pion/muon energy into the visible
channel, together with the radiation efficiency in the adopted environment,
which together account for the remaining orders. The phase-space factors
$\rho_\pi,\rho_\mu$ are of order unity over most of the
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range and become significant only near the kinematic
endpoint, so they are not counted as a source of orders of magnitude.
Applying the experimental bound $|U_{\mu N}|^2 \lesssim10^{-4}$ (the representative
value over this range) adds four more orders of magnitude, bringing the
total suppression to seven to eight orders. Even if the mixing angle were
set to unity, the channel would remain three to four orders of magnitude
short of the data.

Two qualifications limit the scope of this numerical claim but do not
motivate treating the present calculation as evidence for a detectable HNL
signal. First, the experimental bound on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ is mass dependent, and
the value quoted for a particular low-mass interval should not be
extrapolated unchanged over the entire \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range,
although the difference is minor. Second, an accurate observable flux from
a finite external region must account for the probability of decay within
that region; the present treatment instead assumes that all HNLs deposit
their visible products in the chosen zone, which makes the estimate
optimistic rather than fully propagated. A more detailed discussion of
these points is given in Sec.~\ref{sec:conclusions}. The comparison in
Fig.~(\ref{fig:gamma_show_compare}) uses the Fermi-LAT point-source upper
limits.\cite{Acciari_2019}.

% \textbf{Quantitative status of the constraint.} 
The constraint obtained
here is \emph{exclusionary}: in the range
$m_N\simeq\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV}$, even with the largest mixing allowed by
experiment ($|U_{\mu N}|^2 \simeq10^{-4}$), the predicted flux remains seven to eight
orders of magnitude too low, so that the channel is excluded as an
explanation of the $\gamma$-ray excess. This does not change the laboratory
upper limit on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$, but it rules out the channel as an explanation over
this mass range.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
In this article we have detailed the calculation of the production and
decay of HNLs mixed with the muon neutrino sector, and applied it to the
multi-messenger data of NGC 1068. For $\pi^\pm \to N + \mu^\pm$ and
$\mu^\pm \to N + e^\pm + \bar{\nu}_e$, we computed the HNL spectra
separated by polarization, boosted them to the laboratory frame while
tracking the mass-dependent helicity redistribution, and then derived the
electron/positron spectrum from the visible decay
$N \to \nu_\mu + e^+ + e^-$ for each initial polarization. Adopting a
representative external radiation environment for NGC 1068, we obtained an
HNL-associated $\gamma$-ray flux that is at least \numrange{7}{8} orders of
magnitude below the current observational limits once the experimental
bound on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ is applied. Even with the mixing angle set to unity, the
predicted flux remains three to four orders of magnitude below the data.
The weakly coupled HNL channel is therefore decisively excluded as the
primary mechanism for the anomalous $\gamma$-ray excess of this source.

The robustness of this exclusion rests on two points, discussed
qualitatively in Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison} and made explicit here. First,
the experimental bound on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$ is mass dependent; the value
$|U_{\mu N}|^2 \lesssim 10^{-4}$ quoted for \qty{3}{MeV} $< m_N <$ \qty{19.5}{MeV}
should not be extrapolated unchanged over the entire
\qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV} range. Over this range the bound is slightly weaker,
but the change is minor and does not affect the order-of-magnitude
conclusion. Second, an accurate observable flux from a finite external
region must account for the probability that an HNL decays within that
region, which depends on its energy, mass, and mixing. The present
calculation instead assumes that all relevant HNLs deposit their visible
decay products in the chosen zone; this makes the estimate optimistic
rather than a fully propagated flux prediction. Both qualifications limit
the quantitative reach of the numbers quoted above, but neither reverses
the conclusion: relaxing them can only reduce the predicted flux further,
so the exclusion is conservative.

The exclusion itself is informative. It removes the simplest weakly coupled
transport channel for the NGC 1068 $\gamma$-ray excess, thereby sharpening
the case for alternative explanations, and it converts the multi-messenger
data of this source into an \emph{exclusionary constraint} on the HNL
parameter space that is independent of, and complementary to, laboratory
bounds. % 【Fix 5】
The total suppression of seven to eight orders of magnitude decomposes into
four orders from the mixing angle (at the experimental bound $10^{-4}$) and
three to four orders from $BR\simeq0.11$ together with the HNL energy
fraction and the radiation efficiency of the source environment (see
Sec.~\ref{sec:comparison}). Even at the experimental upper bound on the
mixing, $BR$ together with the energy fraction and efficiency still suppress
the signal; this does not change the laboratory
upper limit on $|U_{\mu N}|^2$, but it rules out the channel as an explanation.

The anomaly therefore needs to be explained through a different process,
most likely a refined model of the AGN environment on which current
electromagnetic cascade calculations are based \cite{murase2020hidden}.
Alternatively, energy transport mediated by other weakly interacting
particles remains a possibility. The polarization-resolved framework
developed here is not specific to this conclusion: it applies to any source
in which HNLs are produced in hadronic decay chains, and it can be reused
for other HNL mass windows and for other weakly coupled neutral particles
whose polarization affects their visible decay products. In this sense the
negative result obtained here is accompanied by a controlled and reusable
tool for future multi-messenger tests of BSM physics.
