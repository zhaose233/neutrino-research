\begin{abstract}
  Recently, the IceCube detector has identified the AGN NGC 1068 as a
  point source of high-energy neutrinos, the $\gamma$-ray flux
  exceeds the prediction of electromagnetic cascade models. This suggests
  some of the $\gamma$ ray energy can be transported outside without being
  attenuated. In this paper, we propose Heavy Neutral Leptons (HNLs) as
  an explanation for this observation. We first review the origin of the HNL framework
  theory, calculate the phenomenological behavior of certain processes
  related to $p-\gamma$ and $p-p$ neutrino production, and use it to
  explain the $\gamma$ ray observation of AGN NGC 1068, and several other
  astronomical observations.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{HNL; Inverse Seesaw; multi-messenger; NGC 1068}

\section{Introduction}

As advancements are made in astronomical observations, different types
of new signals have become observable. Alongside gravitational waves 
and a wider range of electromagnetic signals, neutrino signals are
now observed by IceCube, which has recently spotted AGN NGC 1068 as
a point source of neutrino
flux around at energies around $\qty{1}{TeV}$ \cite{icecube2022evidence},
and several other experiments, such as
KM3NeT/ARCA, while still under
construction, have observed a neutrino signal which possibly has energy
as high as \qty{220}{PeV}.\cite{km3net2025observation}

Furthermore, neutrinos have always been peculiar in Standard Model (SM),
because its tiny mass, hence extremely weak coupling to the Higgs field, is
considered somewhat unnatural. This has led the neutrino and the origin of 
its small mass to be considered a gateway to Beyond Standard Model (BSM) physics, 
with Heavy Neutral Leptons (HNLs) serving as viable dark matter candidates.
Numerous terrestrial experiments are dedicated to searching for possible signals
coming from BSM theories.\cite{PhysRevLett.103.241802} Although no definite
signal has been observed, they do provide a solid constraint on the parameter
space of possible BSM theories, for HNL with $\qty{3}{MeV} < m_N < \qty{19.5}{MeV}$, the
mixing angle with muon neutrino is constrained roughly as $|U_{\mu N}|^2 \lesssim 10^{-4}$.
\cite{bryman2019constraints}

Moreover, the observed $\gamma$-ray flux of NGC 1068 exceeds theoretical
predictions based on corona cascade models around \qty{10}{GeV}. This suggests
that the hidden core of NGC 1068 has some process to transport more
electromagnetic energy out. Axions are one candidate \cite{pant2024constraining};
another possibility is HNLs, which are discussed in this paper. Before NGC 1068,
there are already attempts to combine multi-messenger observations
to constrain BSM theories.\cite{PhysRevLett.58.1490, PhysRevD.94.085012, Magill_2018}

This paper is structured as follows. In Section , we outline the basics of HNL
theory originating from neutrino mass, and calculate its phenomenology
properties, while taking polarization into account. In Section , we investigate
the multi-messenger observation results of NGC 1068, try to explain the
exceeding $\gamma$-ray flux. Conclusions are in Section .

\section{ISS Model}

To explain the tiny neutrino masses, several mechanisms known as seesaw
mechanisms have been proposed. For Type-I Seesaw model, we have
\begin{equation}
  m_\nu = \frac{m_D^2}{M_R}, \; m_N = M_R, \; |U|^2 = \left( \frac{m_D}{M_R} \right)^2
\end{equation}
where $m_D$ is the Dirac mass of neutrino, and $M_R$ is the Majorana mass
of the added right-handed neutrino field. For a sufficiently natural $m_D$
and $m_R$, the mixing angle $|U|^2$ is always too small which makes observation
impossible. However, by introducing another fermionic field, the mass
matrix becomes
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \overline{\nu_L} & \overline{\nu_R^c} & \overline{S^c}
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    0   & m_D & 0   \\
    m_D & 0   & M   \\
    0   & M   & \mu
  \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix}
    \nu_L^c \\
    \nu_R   \\
    S
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
The new parameter $\mu$ stands for potential lepton number violation, which should
be small. The basic properties of the neutrino and a pseudo-Dirac fermion pair,
which can be treated
as a fermion because of the small $\mu$, are
\begin{equation}
  m_\nu = \mu \frac{m_D^2}{M^2}, \; m_N = M, \; |U|^2 = \frac{m_\nu}{\mu}
\end{equation}

\section{HNL Production}

Such HNL particles interact only through their mixing with SM neutrinos,
which implies that processes producing SM neutrinos will also produce HNLs,
provided they are kinematically allowed.

\subsection{Pion decay}
Under AGN conditions, $p-p$ and $p-\gamma$ processes are thought
to be the origin of high energy cosmic rays.\cite{Murase_2023} Those
processes create a large number of pions, awhose decays subsequently
produce high-energy cosmic rays, including neutrinos and $\gamma$-rays.
\cite{kelner2006energy}

After introducing HNL particles mixing with SM neutrino, charged pions can decay
into HNL particles, intermediated by W bosons.

By slightly modifying the process of SM pion decay, we find that the decay width
to HNL is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\pi \to \mu N}}{\Gamma_{\pi \to \mu \nu}} =
  |U_{\mu}|^2 \times \rho_\pi (m_N) \label{eq:pion_HNL_rho}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
   & \rho_\pi (m_N) = \frac{(m_\pi^2 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}}{m_\mu^2 (m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2)^2} \label{eq:rho_pi} \\
   & \lambda(x, y, z) = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 2xy - 2yz - 2zx
\end{align}
It is useful to determine the helicity of the HNL particle, since
it will play a role in the angular dependence when working on the
decay of HNL. In the pion rest frame we have
\begin{equation}
  % \Gamma_{\pi \to N}^{(s)} \propto (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) m_\pi^2 - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2 - s \cdot (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}
  {\Gamma_{(s)}} \sim {(m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) m_\pi^2 - (m_\mu^2 - m_N^2)^2 - s(m_\mu^2 - m_N^2) \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}} \label{eq:hnl_hel_pion}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the helicity of HNL, and $s=+1$ for right-handed polarized HNL particle.

  \centerline{\includegraphics{../julia_plots/pion_decay_hel_br.pdf}}
  \caption{Eq.~\ref{eq:hnl_hel_pion} for different $s$. In pion decay,
  right-handed HNLs are more likely to be produced close to the phase
  space boundary.}
  \label{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist}
  \alttext{$\cos\theta_r$ when calculating HNL spectrum from pion decay.}
\end{figure}

There are no differences in angular dependence for different helicity end
states, since pions are unpolarized. After boosting to the lab frame,
we must account for the polarization change on the HNL particles,
thus for a pion spectrum $J(E_\pi)$, we get
\begin{equation}
  % Q_{N}^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\frac{E_N}{\lambda^+}}^{\frac{E_N}{\lambda^-}} J(E_\pi) |U_{\mu}|^2 \rho_\pi \times r_{(s)}(E_\pi, E_N) \times \frac{d E_\pi}{\lambda_{max} E_\pi} \label{eq:hnl_spec_from_pion}
  Q_{N}^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\lambda^-}^{\lambda^+} J(\frac{E_N}{x}) |U_{\mu}|^2 \rho_\pi \times r_{(s)}(x, E_N) \times \frac{d x}{(\lambda^+ - \lambda^-) \cdot x} \label{eq:hnl_spec_from_pion}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
   & \lambda^{\pm}       = \frac{m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2 + m_N^2 \pm \sqrt{\lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}}{2 m_\pi^2}       \\
   & \cos\theta_r        = \frac{x \, (m_\pi^2 - m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) - 2 m_N^2}{x \, \sqrt{(1 - \frac{m_N^2}{E_N^2}) \, \lambda(m_\pi^2, m_\mu^2, m_N^2)}} \label{eq:cos_theta_r_pion}\\
   & r_{(s)}(x, E_N) = \frac{\Gamma_{(s)}\frac{1 + \cos\theta_r}{2} + \Gamma_{(-s)}\frac{1 - \cos\theta_r}{2}}{\Gamma_{(s)} + \Gamma_{(-s)}}
\end{align}
Here we assume $E_\pi \gg m_\pi$ to simplify the boost process.
This resembles the SM neutrino case\cite{kelner2006energy}, with
modifications arising from HNL mass and helicity separation.

  \centerline{\includegraphics{../julia_plots/pion_decay_lr.pdf}}
  \caption{Eq.~\ref{eq:cos_theta_r_pion} visualized with different
  $m_N$ values. Eq.~\ref{eq:cos_theta_r_pion} determines how much
  helicity flip will happen during Lorentz boost. When $m_N$
  is small $\cos\theta_r \approx 1$, combined with Fig.~\ref{fig:pion_rest_helicity_dist},
  HNLs produced are almost left-handed; as $m_N$
  approaches the edge of phase space, the helicity flip is more
  significant and right-handed HNLs cannot be ignored.}
  \label{fig:pion_spec_helicity_flip}
  \alttext{$\cos\theta_r$ when calculating HNL spectrum from pion decay.}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Muon decay}
Muons will decay into HNL particles through through a process similar
to their decay into $\nu_\mu$. We find the decay width of $\mu^- \to N e^- \bar{\nu}_e$ has the same
form as the process of $\tau$ decay which has been studied
\cite{Bondarenko_2018}, since this process is symmetric between
$\mu$ and $\tau$, which is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Gamma_{\mu \to N}}{\Gamma_{\mu \to \nu_\mu}} = |U_{\mu}|^2 \times \rho_\mu(m_N)
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  \rho_\mu(m_N) = - r_N^8 + 8 r_N^6 - 24 r_N^4 \ln(r_N) - 8 r_N^2 + 1 \label{eq:rho_mu},\;\;
  r_N = m_N / m_\mu
\end{equation}
The helicity-dependent spectrum of the HNL in the muon rest frame is given by
\begin{align}
  \frac{d\Gamma}{dE} = \frac{G_F^2}{24 \pi^3} |U_\mu|^2 \times |\mathbf{p}| \cdot [ & 3 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) E - 4 m_\mu E^2 - 2 m_N^2 m_\mu \nonumber                   \\
                                                                                    & - s \cdot |\mathbf{p}| (m_N^2 + 3 m_\mu^2 - 4 m_\mu E) ] \label{eq:mu_HNL_spec}
\end{align}
Several examples are plotted in Fig.~\ref{fig:muon_decay_spec}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centerline{\includegraphics{../julia_plots/muon_decay_spectrum.pdf}}
  \caption{Energy spectrum of HNL when muons decay into HNLs in muon rest frame.
  Normalized under $m_N = 0$, which
  basically resembles SM neutrino, with no right-handed product produced.
  When $m_N$ increases, shape of the spectrum stays roughly the same,
  but the max energy increases while the total decay
  width decreases, yielding a non-negligible fraction of right-handed HNLs.}
  \label{fig:muon_decay_spec}
  \alttext{HNL Spectrum of Muon Decay Separated by Helicity}
\end{figure}

Although one could retain the muon polarization and compute the angular
dependence of the resulting HNL helicity states, we neglect muon
polarization here under the assumption that the turbulent magnetic
fields in the AGN completely depolarize the muons, in favor of simplifying
our calculation.

% For different HNL energy, we have
% \begin{align}
%   \frac{d\Gamma_{(s)}}{dE} \sim \; &3 (m_\mu^2 + m_N^2) E - 4 m_\mu E^2 - 2m_N^2 m_\mu \nonumber \\
%   &- s \cdot |\mathbf{p}| (m_N^2 + 3 m_\mu^2 - 4 m_\mu E) 
% \end{align}
For a given muon spectrum, we have

\begin{align}
  Q_N^{(s)}(E_N) = \int_{\frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}^{1} & \frac{d y}{2y} \, Q_\mu(\frac{E_N}{y}) |U_\mu|^2 \rho_\mu \nonumber \cdot \int_{x^-}^{x^+}  \frac{d x}{\sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}} \nonumber \\
  & \cdot \left(\frac{1 + \cos\theta_r}{2} g_N^{(s)}(x) + \frac{1 - \cos\theta_r}{2} g_N^{(-s)}(x)\right) \label{eq:muon_lab_HNL_spec}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  &x^+ = \frac{m_N^2 + m_\mu^2}{2 m_\mu^2}, \; x^- = \frac{1}{2} \left( y + \frac{1}{y} \cdot \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}\right) \\
  &\cos\theta_0 = \frac{y - x}{\sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}}} \\
  &\cos\theta_r = \frac{1}{y \, \sqrt{1 - \frac{m_N^2}{E_N^2}}}\left( \sqrt{x^2 - \frac{m_N^2}{m_\mu^2}} + x \cos\theta_0\right)
\end{align}
where $g^{(s)}_N(x)$ is normalized as
\begin{equation}
  \int^{x^+}_{\frac{m_N}{m_\mu}} dx \sum_{s=\pm 1} g^{(s)}_N(x) = 1
\end{equation}

\section{HNL Decay}
Straightforwardly, HNL particles in the ISS model will exclusively
decay into other leptons. Possible products are $\nu_\mu e^- e^+$
and $\nu_\mu \nu_l \bar{\nu}_l$. These processes have been well studied\cite{Bondarenko_2018};
their decay widths are given by
\begin{align}
  \Gamma_{N \to 3\nu}          & = \frac{G_F^2 m_N^5}{192 \pi^3} \times |U_\mu|^2 \label{eq:HNL_3nu_decay}               \\
  \Gamma_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} & = \frac{G_F^2 m_N^5}{192 \pi^3} \times |U_\mu|^2 \times C_1^e \label{eq:HNL_nuee_decay}
\end{align}
where
\begin{equation}
  C_1^e = \frac{1}{4}\left(1 - 4 \sin^2 \theta_W + 8 \sin^4 \theta_W\right)
\end{equation}
To simplify the problem, we neglect the electron mass. The $3\nu$ final state
includes all neutrino flavor combinations.
Thus the branching ratio of HNL particle undergoing a visible decay
is
\begin{equation}
  BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} = \frac{C_1^e}{1 + C_1^e} \approx 0.11
\end{equation}
This results in substantial energy loss, as only approximately
one-tenth of the HNL decays are observable.
The angular and energy distributions of electrons/positrons are
\begin{align}
  g_e^{(1)}(x, \cos{\theta}) = & \frac{8}{C_1^e} \cdot x^2 \cdot \{ [(4 - 16 s_W^2 + 64 s_W^4) x - (1 - 4 s_W^2 + 28 s_W^4)] \cos{\theta} \nonumber   \\
                               & - (4 - 16 s_W^2 + 64 s_W^4) x + (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 36 s_W^4) \} \label{eq:g_e_p2}                                       \\
  g_e^{(2)}(x, \cos{\theta}) = & \frac{16}{C_1^e} \cdot x^2 \cdot \{ [(6 - 24 s_W^2 + 32 s_W^4) x - (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 14 s_W^4)] \cos{\theta} \nonumber \\
                               & - (6 - 24 s_W^2 + 32 s_W^4) x + (3 - 12 s_W^2 + 18 s_W^4) \} \label{eq:g_e_p3}
\end{align}
where  $x = E_e / m_N$, $s_W = \sin{\theta_W}$. $\theta$ is the
angle between the out-going electron and initial HNL spin.

To get the total electron spectrum from a given HNL spectrum, we
integrate over the HNL spectrum to obtain
\begin{align}
  Q_e^{(Lab)}(E) = BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}} \cdot \int_{E}^{+\infty} &d E_N \, Q_N^{(s)}(E_N) \nonumber \\
  &\cdot \int_{\frac{E}{2 E_N}}^{\frac{1}{2}}  \frac{d x}{x E_N} \, \sum_{i=1,2} g_e^{(i)}(x, s (\frac{E}{x E_N} - 1))
\end{align}
Combining with \ref{eq:g_e_p2} and \ref{eq:g_e_p3}, the remaining
integral is
\begin{align}
  Q_e^{(Lab)}(E) = &BR_{N \to \nu e \bar{e}}  \nonumber\\
                   &\cdot \int_{0}^{1} \frac{d y}{3y} \, Q_N^{(s)}(\frac{E}{y}) [16 y^3 - 27 y^2 + 11 -  s \cdot (32 y^3 - 63 y^2 + 36 y - 5) ]
\end{align}
where $y=E/E_N$, and $\theta_W$ no longer appears in this expression.

\section{HNL in NGC 1068}
According to IceCube observations, NGC 1068 has been identified as
a neutrino point source, and the dominant channel of neutrino production is
charged pion decay.\cite{murase2020hidden} By combining the
analytical neutrino spectrum from pion decays \cite{kelner2006energy}
with theoretical models of NGC 1068 \cite{murase2020hidden},
we adopt the following parameterization as an approximation of the pion spectrum:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_{\pi^\pm}(E) = \Phi_0 \cdot \left( \frac{E}{\qty{1}{GeV}}\right)^{-\Gamma} \cdot e^{-(E / E_c)^\beta} \label{eq:pion_spec_form}
\end{equation}
where the best fit, which is visualized in Fig.~\ref{fig:pion_spec_fit_show}, is
\begin{equation}
  \Phi_0 = \qty{4.7e-12}{GeV^{-1}.cm^{-2}.s^{-1}}, \Gamma = 0.1, E_c = \qty{6.2}{GeV} , \beta=0.3 \label{eq:pion_spec_par}
\end{equation}
By setting $|U_\mu|^2 = 1$ and $m_N = \qtyrange{10}{30}{MeV}$, we find that the typical
lifetime of HNL particle is in the sub-second region, which
means when combined with the actual $|U_\mu|^2 \lessapprox  10^{-2}$
the lifetime is longer than that of the muon by \numrange{7}{8} orders of magnitude; hence,
it can escape into the outer regions of AGN core. We refer to the
parameters of the AGN torus model\cite{Lopez_Rodriguez_2015}. For our approximation
we set the magnetic field strength to \qty{139}{mG}, dust temperature to \qty{1500}{K},
ambient density to \qty{2.30e5}{cm^{-3}}, bolometric luminosity to \qty{9.55e44}{erg . s^{-1}},
and assume the electron-photon conversion happens at about \qty{0.4}{pc} away
from the AGN core. We use GAMERA to calculate the radiation
generated by an input particle spectrum.\cite{Hahn:2016CO} The result is
shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:gamma_show_compare}.

\section{Conclusions}
In this article, we carefully calculated the production and decay
processes of HNL particle, assuming it has a mixing angle with muon
neutrino. For $\pi^\pm \to N + \mu^\pm$ and $\mu^\pm \to N + e^\pm + \bar{\nu}_e$,
we calculated the HNL spectrum separated by polarization
% (Eq.~\ref{eq:hnl_spec_from_pion} and \ref{eq:muon_lab_HNL_spec})
, and
applied Lorentz boost to the lab frame while taking polarization
in account. Then we derived the electron/positron spectrum originating
from HNL decay with different initial polarizations. We applied this
approach to calculate the possible observables of NGC 1068, assuming
that a small mixing angle does exist. While the result suggests it is highly
unlikely that this HNL decay route will contribute to any possible
observations, the phenomenological framework remains valuable for future studies.
